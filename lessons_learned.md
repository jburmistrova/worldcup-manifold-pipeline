# Lessons Learned

Generalizable takeaways from this project — distinct from `docs/decisions/` (which records *what* was decided and why, for this project specifically). This is about transferable methodology worth carrying into other work, including interview conversations about how I debug, not just what I built.

## Test your explanation, don't just believe the plausible one (2026-07-31)

**What happened:** found that Manifold's search results seemed to be missing a whole category of markets (sports-linked ones). The obvious, plausible explanation was already sitting right there — we'd *just* fixed a real pagination-instability bug (ADR-0006, duplicate markets from unstable relevance ranking). It would have been easy to assume "same bug, different symptom" and move on.

**What actually happened instead:** tested that exact hypothesis directly — ran 5 independent full pagination sweeps and checked whether the missing markets showed up in some sweeps but not others (which is what instability would look like). They didn't. Every sweep returned the identical result, zero variance. The obvious explanation was wrong.

That "failed" test wasn't wasted effort — ruling out the plausible-but-wrong explanation was what forced isolating the real variable (page size, `limit`), which was the actual cause (ADR-0007). If I'd shipped a fix based on the first explanation (more retries, more sweeps), it wouldn't have changed anything, and the real bug — silently missing 37% of the dataset — would still be there, undetected, because the fix would have "worked" by coincidence of not breaking anything, while not actually fixing the real problem.

**The generalizable lesson:** when a bug looks like one you've already seen, that's exactly when to be most suspicious of pattern-matching instead of testing. A quick, direct test that isolates one variable at a time (here: hold everything constant, vary only `limit`) is cheap compared to shipping a fix that doesn't address the real cause. "I ran a test and it disproved my first theory" is a *good* debugging story, not a failed one — it's evidence of a real investigation, not a guess that happened to work.

## Two numbers sharing a name aren't the same comparison (2026-07-31)

**What happened:** compared this project's Brier score to Manifold's published platform-wide number (0.1748) and got 0.0185 — dramatically, suspiciously better. It was tempting to report that as the headline result. Instead, dug into *why* the gap was so large, and found the two numbers weren't measuring the same thing at all: Manifold's is trade-weighted (one row per trade, using the average of the market's probability just before and just after that trade, compared against the market's eventual resolution, across the market's entire trading lifetime) and restricted to `BINARY` markets with 15+ traders. Ours was one row per resolved market or answer, using its probability at *resolution time only* — a single snapshot, not a lifetime of trades — and it included `MULTIPLE_CHOICE` answers Manifold's number excludes entirely. Excluding a dominant "easy" bucket brought our number to 0.0437, still nowhere near a real fix, because the underlying unit of analysis was still wrong.

**What actually happened instead:** rebuilt the comparison from the unit of analysis up instead of tuning the existing number. Wrote [`mart_trade_calibration`](../dbt/models/marts/mart_trade_calibration.sql): one row per real trade, `BINARY` markets only, 15+ distinct traders, predicted probability = `(prob_before + prob_after) / 2` — Manifold's own stated definition, quoted from their calibration page, not a guess at it. That produced a materially different number: **0.1305**, not 0.0185. Still better than Manifold's 0.1748, but by a believable margin instead of a suspicious one.

**The generalizable lesson:** a benchmark comparison is only as valid as the match between the two things being compared, and a shared metric *name* ("Brier score") is not evidence of a shared metric *definition*. Before trusting a number that looks flattering (or damning), check what population, grain, and time-window it was actually computed over — not just what it's called. A 10x gap against a credible external benchmark is much more often a sign the two sides are measuring different things than a sign of a real 10x effect. Read the other side's actual methodology and replicate their unit of analysis exactly before drawing a conclusion from the comparison — "I found a suspiciously good number and went looking for why" is the right instinct, and it's a good story to be able to tell in an interview, not something to gloss over in favor of the flattering headline.
